Discrete Probability Distributions

How chance spreads itself across a handful of countable outcomes, and the few shapes it keeps choosing.

Stage 0

Foundations

What is a discrete distribution?

Some questions have answers you can count: the face a die shows, how many heads in 10 flips, how many emails arrive in an hour. You can list every possible answer: 0, 1, 2, 3…

A discrete probability distribution is that list, plus a number next to each answer telling you how likely it is. The rule that gives those numbers is called the probability mass function, or PMF, written \(P(X = x)\).

Every bar is between 0 and 1No outcome can be less than impossible 0 or more than certain 1.
All bars add up to 1Something has to happen, so the chances share one whole: Σ P(x) = 1.

Two numbers that summarise any distribution

Mean (μ) is the balance point. Imagine the bars are weights on a ruler: the black triangle is where the ruler balances. Drag a bar on the right up and watch the triangle slide right.

Standard deviation (σ) measures spread: how far outcomes typically land from the mean. The shaded band is μ ± σ. Pile everything onto one face and it shrinks to nothing; push weight to both ends and it widens.

The CDF (cumulative distribution function) is the running total of the bars, read left to right. It answers questions like "what's the chance of rolling 4 or less?" It always climbs in steps from 0 to 1.

Definitions
$$\mu = E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(x)$$ $$\sigma^2 = \sum_x (x-\mu)^2\,P(x)$$ $$F(x) = P(X \le x)$$
In words

Mean: each outcome times its chance, added up.

Variance σ²: average squared distance from the mean. σ is its square root.

CDF: add up every bar up to x.

Stage 1

Discrete Uniform Distribution

Every outcome equally likely

The simplest distribution of all: \(n\) outcomes, and no favourites. Each one gets the same slice, \(1/n\). A fair die is uniform with \(n = 6\); a fair lottery ball draw is uniform with \(n\) = number of balls.

A count is Discrete Uniform only when both of these hold:

1 · A fixed list of n outcomesYou can write down every possible result in advance: faces 1 to \(n\), tickets 1 to 20.
Breaks if: there's no upper limit, like the number of emails you'll get today. That's closer to Poisson.
2 · No favouritesEvery outcome has exactly the same chance, \(1/n\).
Breaks if: the die is loaded, or you add up two dice: a total of 7 is six times as likely as a total of 2.

Stage 2

Bernoulli Distribution

One try, two outcomes

A Bernoulli trial is any single experiment with a yes/no answer: did the coin land heads, did the customer click, did the free throw go in. We call "yes" a success and score it 1; "no" is a failure, scored 0.

One number controls everything: \(p\), the chance of success. The failure bar is whatever is left, \(1-p\).

That rule is the Bernoulli PMF. It fits in one line, \(P(X = x) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\): put in \(x = 1\) and you get \(p\); put in \(x = 0\) and you get \(1-p\).

A random quantity is Bernoulli only when both of these hold:

1 · Exactly one tryA single shot, a single flip, a single customer.
Breaks if: you take 10 shots and count how many go in. That's Binomial.
2 · Two outcomes, scored 1 and 0Success counts as 1, failure as 0, and nothing else can happen.
Breaks if: the result has more levels, like a die's 1 to 6, unless you collapse it to "six / not six".
Example: a basketball player makes 70% of free throws. One shot is Bernoulli with \(p = 0.7\).

Stage 3

Binomial Distribution

Count the successes in n tries

Now repeat the Bernoulli trial \(n\) times. Same \(p\) every time, and no trial affects another. Then count the successes. That count, a number from 0 to \(n\), follows the Binomial distribution.

A count is Binomial only when all four of these hold:

1 · Fixed number of trials\(n\) is decided before you start: 10 free throws, 20 bulbs, 30 visitors.
Breaks if: you keep going "until the 3rd success". That's Negative Binomial.
2 · Two outcomes per trialEach trial is either a success (1) or a failure (0). Nothing in between.
Breaks if: a die roll is scored 1–6 rather than "six / not six".
3 · Same p every timeSuccess has chance \(p\) on every trial, so failure always has chance \(1-p\).
Breaks if: the player gets tired and their \(p\) drops shot by shot.
4 · Independent trialsOne trial's outcome doesn't change the next one's chances.
Breaks if: you draw cards without putting them back. Each draw changes the deck.
Example: the same player takes 10 free throws. How many go in? Binomial with \(n = 10,\ p = 0.7\).

Reading the graph

Each bar is the chance of one exact count. The tallest bar is the most likely count, and it sits close to the mean \(np\). Move the sliders and watch how the shape responds:

When p goes up ↑The hump slides right: more successes become likely. At \(p = 0.5\) it is perfectly symmetric. Near 0 or 1 it gets squashed against a wall and becomes lopsided, with a long tail on the open side.
When n goes up ↑The hump also slides right (the mean \(np\) grows), and it spreads out, because \(\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}\) grows too. Each bar gets shorter since the same total of 1 is shared by more bars, and the shape tends toward a smooth bell curve.

Where the formula comes from

Take \(n = 5\) and ask for exactly \(k = 2\) successes. One way that happens is S S F F F. Its probability is \(p \cdot p \cdot (1-p)(1-p)(1-p) = p^2(1-p)^3\).

But S F S F F, F F F S S, and others work too. Every arrangement has the same probability, so we just count the arrangements: "5 choose 2" \(= \binom{5}{2} = 10\). Multiply and you have the formula.

That is the Binomial PMF. It gives the chance of exactly \(k\) successes, for any \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\).

PMF
$$P(X = k) = \underbrace{\binom{n}{k}}_{\text{arrangements}}\; \underbrace{p^k (1-p)^{n-k}}_{\text{one arrangement}}$$
CDF
$$F(k) = P(X \le k) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \binom{n}{i} p^i (1-p)^{n-i}$$
Summary
$$\mu = np,\qquad \sigma^2 = np(1-p)$$

With \(n = 1\) this is exactly Bernoulli.

\(p\)

\(\binom{n}{k}\)

The CDF: "at most" and "at least"

The PMF answers "exactly \(k\)". The CDF \(F(k) = P(X \le k)\) answers "at most \(k\)": add up every bar from 0 to \(k\). There's no shortcut formula; it really is just that sum. Hover the cumulative chart above to watch it add up.

For "at least \(k\)", flip it around. Everything that isn't "at most \(k-1\)" is "at least \(k\)":

$$P(X \ge k) = 1 - F(k-1)$$
Worked example: 10 free throws, \(p = 0.7\) (the chart's starting setting).
At most 7 go in: \(F(7) = P(0) + P(1) + \dots + P(7) \approx 0.617\). Hover step 7 on the cumulative chart to check.
At least 8 go in: \(P(X \ge 8) = 1 - F(7) \approx 1 - 0.617 = 0.383\). That's the same as adding the three right-hand bars, \(0.233 + 0.121 + 0.028\).

Stage 4

Negative Binomial Distribution

How many tries until the r-th success?

Binomial fixes the number of tries and counts the successes. The Negative Binomial flips the question around: fix the number of successes you want, \(r\), and count how many tries it takes to get there.

Since you can't succeed more times than you try, the answer is always at least \(r\). There is no upper limit, though: with bad luck it could take forever, so the bars go on to the right without end (they just get too small to see).

A count is Negative Binomial only when all four of these hold:

1 · Fixed number of successes\(r\) is decided before you start: until the 3rd deal, until the 1st six.
Breaks if: the number of tries is fixed instead, like "make 20 calls". That's Binomial.
2 · Two outcomes per tryEach try is a success or a failure. Nothing in between.
Breaks if: a call can also end in "maybe, call me next week", and you count that differently.
3 · Same p every timeEvery try has the same chance of success \(p\).
Breaks if: the salesperson warms up and gets better as the day goes on.
4 · Independent triesOne try's result doesn't change the next one's chances.
Breaks if: a string of rejections makes the salesperson nervous, which lowers their chances on the next call.
Example: a salesperson closes 20% of calls. How many calls until they close 3 deals? Negative Binomial with \(r = 3,\ p = 0.2\).

Reading the graph

There's a wall on the left at \(x = r\) and no limit on the right, so the shape always has a long tail trailing off to the right. Move the sliders and watch:

When p goes up ↑The hump slides left and gets narrower: easy successes mean fewer tries. The mean \(r/p\) shrinks toward \(r\). At \(p = 1\) every try succeeds, so everything sits on one bar at \(x = r\).
When r goes up ↑The hump slides right (the mean \(r/p\) grows) and spreads out, because \(\sigma^2 = r(1-p)/p^2\) grows too. With a big \(r\) the wall at the left matters less and the shape turns into a bell.

Special case: the Geometric distribution

Press Set r = 1. Now you're asking "how many tries until the first success?" That special case has its own name, the Geometric distribution. Its bars always start tallest at 1 and shrink step by step, each one \((1-p)\) times the one before. Small \(p\) means a long, slow tail: rare events can keep you waiting.

Where the formula comes from

For the \(r\)-th success to land exactly on try \(x\), two things must happen: try \(x\) itself is a success, and the earlier \(x - 1\) tries contain exactly \(r - 1\) successes in any order. Counting those orders gives \(\binom{x-1}{r-1}\).

PMF
$$P(X = x) = \binom{x-1}{r-1}\, p^r (1-p)^{x-r}$$

for \(x = r,\ r+1,\ r+2,\dots\)

CDF
$$F(x) = P(X \le x) = \sum_{i=r}^{x} \binom{i-1}{r-1} p^r (1-p)^{i-r}$$

With \(r = 1\) (Geometric) the sum collapses to \(F(x) = 1 - (1-p)^x\): the chance that not all of the first \(x\) tries fail.

Summary
$$\mu = \frac{r}{p},\qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}$$

Some books count only the failures before the r-th success; that's this same distribution shifted left by \(r\).

\(x\)
the number of tries it takes to reach the \(r\)-th success (\(r\) or more)
\(r\)
how many successes you're waiting for
\(p\)
chance of success on any single try
\(\binom{x-1}{r-1}\)

Stage 5

Poisson Distribution

How many events happen in a fixed window?

Some things happen at random moments, at some steady average rate: customers walking into a shop, typos on a page, meteors in the night sky. Pick a window (an hour, a page, a square metre) and count the events inside it. That count follows a Poisson distribution.

Only one number is needed: \(\lambda\) (lambda), the average count per window. There are no "trials" and no \(p\), just a rate.

A count is Poisson when all four of these hold:

1 · A fixed windowYou count events inside a set stretch of time or space: one hour, one page, one square metre.
Breaks if: you wait until the next event happens. That waiting time is a continuous quantity, not a count.
2 · One event at a timeTwo events never land at exactly the same instant.
Breaks if: people arrive in groups, like a bus dropping 30 shoppers at the door at once.
3 · A steady average rateEvents are equally likely at every moment of the window.
Breaks if: you count a whole day's calls with a lunchtime rush. Split the day into windows with their own \(\lambda\).
4 · Independent eventsOne event doesn't make another more or less likely.
Breaks if: one earthquake sets off a cluster of aftershocks.

What λ actually means

The long-run average countIf you watched many, many hours and averaged the counts, you'd get \(\lambda\). Any single hour can still have 0, 2 or 9 events. \(\lambda\) is the average, not a promise.
Rate × window length\(\lambda\) belongs to the window you picked. At 4 calls per hour, a 30-minute window has \(\lambda = 2\) and a 2-hour window has \(\lambda = 8\). Change the window and \(\lambda\) scales with it.
Doesn't have to be wholeCounts are whole numbers, but their average doesn't have to be: \(\lambda = 2.5\) typos per page is perfectly fine.
Sets the centre and the spreadThe mean is \(\lambda\) and the variance is \(\lambda\). A bigger \(\lambda\) moves the hump right and spreads it out, both at once.
Example: a help desk gets on average 4 calls an hour. Calls in the next hour: Poisson with \(\lambda = 4\).

When λ goes up ↑The hump slides right (the mean is \(\lambda\)) and spreads out (\(\sigma = \sqrt{\lambda}\)). Bars get shorter as more counts share the total of 1, and the lopsided shape turns into a symmetric bell.

Why that formula? Slice the hour into tiny pieces

The formula looks like it came from nowhere, but it is just the Binomial in disguise. Take the help desk: \(\lambda = 4\) calls per hour, and ask for exactly \(k = 2\) calls.

Step 1 · Chop the hour into n slicesCut the hour into \(n\) equal slices, say \(n = 3600\) one-second slices. Make them thin enough that two calls never land in the same slice. Then each slice is a yes/no trial: a call arrives in it, or it doesn't.
Step 2 · The chance per slice is p = λ / nThe hour holds \(\lambda = 4\) calls on average, spread over \(n\) slices, so each slice gets a call with chance \(p = \lambda / n = 4/3600 \approx 0.0011\). Check: \(n\) slices × \(\lambda/n\) each \(= \lambda\) calls expected. ✓
Step 3 · That's a Binomial\(n\) independent slices with the same chance \(p\), counting the "yes" slices, is exactly Binomial. Pick which \(k\) slices get a call (\(\binom{n}{k}\) ways), those \(k\) say yes, the other \(n-k\) say no: $$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{k}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k}$$
Step 4 · Make the slices infinitely thin (n → ∞)Real calls can arrive at any instant, not just on the second, so let \(n\) grow without limit and see what each piece turns into: $$\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{n^k} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!\;n^k} \;\to\; \frac{1}{k!}$$ With huge \(n\), each of \(n, n-1, \dots, n-k+1\) is basically \(n\), so the top is about \(n^k\) and cancels. So \(\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{k} \to \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\). $$\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n} \;\to\; e^{-\lambda}$$ This is the famous limit that defines \(e\). It is the chance that all \(n\) slices stay quiet. The leftover \(\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}\) has only \(k\) factors, each almost 1, so it \(\to 1\).
Step 5 · Multiply the pieces $$\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{k}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot e^{-\lambda}$$ That's the Poisson PMF. The \(n\) and the \(p\) have both disappeared, and only the rate \(\lambda\) is left.
Check with numbers (\(\lambda = 4,\ k = 2\)). Poisson gives \(\frac{4^2 e^{-4}}{2!} \approx 0.1465\). The Binomial with \(p = 4/n\) gets there as the slices shrink:
\(n = 10\) slices (6 min each): \(0.1209\)
\(n = 60\) slices (1 min each): \(0.1439\)
\(n = 3600\) slices (1 s each): \(0.1465\)
Likewise \(\left(1-\frac{4}{3600}\right)^{3600} \approx 0.01827\), already very close to \(e^{-4} \approx 0.01832\).

Worked examples

Exams · "exactly"

Guessing on a quiz

A quiz has 10 multiple-choice questions, each with 4 options. You guess every answer at random.

What's the chance you get exactly 3 right?

  1. Check: 10 fixed questions, each right or wrong, \(p = 1/4\) every time, guesses independent. So \(X \sim \text{Bin}(10,\ 0.25)\).
  2. Arrangements: \(\binom{10}{3} = 120\) ways to pick which 3 are right.
  3. One arrangement: \(0.25^3 \times 0.75^7 = 0.015625 \times 0.1335 \approx 0.002086\).
  4. Multiply: \(120 \times 0.002086 \approx 0.250\).
P(X = 3) ≈ 0.250, about 1 in 4
Quality control · "at most"

Faulty bulbs in a box

A factory's bulbs are faulty 5% of the time, independently. A box holds 20 bulbs.

What's the chance the box has at most 1 faulty bulb?

  1. Check: 20 fixed bulbs, each faulty or fine, \(p = 0.05\), independent. So \(X \sim \text{Bin}(20,\ 0.05)\).
  2. "At most 1" is the CDF: \(F(1) = P(0) + P(1)\).
  3. \(P(0) = 0.95^{20} \approx 0.358\)
  4. \(P(1) = \binom{20}{1}\,0.05 \times 0.95^{19} = 20 \times 0.05 \times 0.377 \approx 0.377\)
  5. Add: \(0.358 + 0.377 = 0.736\).
P(X ≤ 1) ≈ 0.736, about 3 boxes in 4
Marketing · "at least"

Will anyone click the ad?

30 people see an ad. Each clicks with chance 10%, independently of the others.

What's the chance at least 1 person clicks?

  1. Check: 30 fixed viewers, click or no click, \(p = 0.1\), independent. So \(X \sim \text{Bin}(30,\ 0.1)\).
  2. "At least 1" means adding 30 bars, so use the flip: \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - F(0) = 1 - P(0)\).
  3. \(P(0) = 0.9^{30} \approx 0.042\): nobody clicks.
  4. \(1 - 0.042 = 0.958\).
P(X ≥ 1) ≈ 0.958, almost certain

Mean & spread

A fair die's balance point

You roll one fair six-sided die. Each face has chance \(1/6\).

What are the mean and the standard deviation of the roll?

  1. Mean: each face times its chance, added up: \(\mu = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6) \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5\).
  2. Squared distances from 3.5: \(6.25,\ 2.25,\ 0.25,\ 0.25,\ 2.25,\ 6.25\), which add to 17.5.
  3. Variance: \(\sigma^2 = 17.5 \times \frac{1}{6} \approx 2.917\).
  4. Standard deviation: \(\sigma = \sqrt{2.917} \approx 1.71\).
μ = 3.5, σ ≈ 1.71
CDF · "at most"

Rolling 4 or less

Same fair die.

What's the chance the roll is 4 or less?

  1. "At most 4" is the CDF: \(F(4) = P(1) + P(2) + P(3) + P(4)\).
  2. Each bar is \(1/6\), and there are 4 of them: \(4 \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6}\).
P(X ≤ 4) = 2/3 ≈ 0.667
Loaded die · "or"

An even number on a loaded die

A loaded die lands on 6 with chance 0.6. The other five faces get 0.08 each. Check: \(5 \times 0.08 + 0.6 = 1\).

What's the chance of an even number?

  1. The even faces are 2, 4 and 6, and only one face can come up, so add their bars.
  2. \(P(2) + P(4) + P(6) = 0.08 + 0.08 + 0.6 = 0.76\).
  3. Bonus: the mean moves to \(0.08 \times (1+2+3+4+5) + 0.6 \times 6 = 1.2 + 3.6 = 4.8\).
P(X is even) = 0.76
Discrete Uniform

Worked examples

Raffle · "at least"

A high ticket number

A raffle has 20 tickets numbered 1 to 20, and one is drawn at random.

What's the chance the winning number is 16 or higher?

  1. Check: 20 outcomes, no favourites. So \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(1, 20)\), each with chance \(1/20 = 0.05\).
  2. 16 or higher means 16, 17, 18, 19, 20: five outcomes.
  3. Add their bars: \(5 \times 0.05 = 0.25\).
P(X ≥ 16) = 5/20 = 0.25
Dice · "multiple of"

A 12-sided die

You roll a fair 12-sided die.

What's the chance the roll is a multiple of 3?

  1. Each face has chance \(1/12\).
  2. The multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12: four faces.
  3. \(4 \times \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{3}\).
P(multiple of 3) = 1/3 ≈ 0.333
Mean & spread

An 8-sided die

You roll a fair 8-sided die.

What are its mean and variance, and how often does it land above the mean?

  1. Mean: \(\mu = \frac{n+1}{2} = \frac{9}{2} = 4.5\).
  2. Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{n^2 - 1}{12} = \frac{63}{12} = 5.25\), so \(\sigma \approx 2.29\).
  3. Above 4.5 means 5, 6, 7 or 8: \(4 \times \frac{1}{8} = 0.5\). A symmetric shape splits evenly around its mean.
μ = 4.5, σ² = 5.25, P(X ≥ 5) = 0.5
Bernoulli

Worked examples

Sports · the failure bar

Missing a free throw

A player makes 70% of free throws. Score one shot as 1 if it goes in, 0 if it misses.

What's the chance of a miss, and what are the mean and variance?

  1. Check: one try, two outcomes. So \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.7)\).
  2. Miss: \(P(X = 0) = 1 - p = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3\).
  3. Mean: \(\mu = p = 0.7\). Variance: \(\sigma^2 = p(1-p) = 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.21\).
P(X = 0) = 0.3 · μ = 0.7 · σ² = 0.21
Quality control · rare event

Is this part faulty?

A machine makes a faulty part 5% of the time. Check one part and score 1 if it's faulty.

What's the chance it's faulty, and how unpredictable is the check?

  1. "Success" here is the thing you're counting, a faulty part, so \(p = 0.05\).
  2. \(P(X = 1) = 0.05\), and \(P(X = 0) = 0.95\).
  3. \(\sigma^2 = 0.05 \times 0.95 = 0.0475\): tiny, because the answer is almost always "fine".
P(X = 1) = 0.05 · σ² = 0.0475
Spread · variance

The least predictable coin

Compare a fair coin (\(p = 0.5\)) with a coin that lands heads 90% of the time.

Which one is harder to predict?

  1. Fair coin: \(\sigma^2 = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\).
  2. 90% coin: \(\sigma^2 = 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.09\).
  3. \(p(1-p)\) is largest at \(p = 0.5\), so the fair coin is the least predictable of all.
σ² = 0.25 (fair) vs 0.09 (90% coin)
Negative Binomial

Worked examples

Sales · "exactly"

The third deal on call 10

A salesperson closes 20% of calls, independently. They keep calling until they close 3 deals.

What's the chance the 3rd deal comes on exactly the 10th call?

  1. Check: fixed number of successes \(r = 3\), \(p = 0.2\), count the tries. So \(X \sim \text{NegBin}(3,\ 0.2)\).
  2. Call 10 is a deal, and the first 9 calls hold exactly 2 deals: \(\binom{9}{2} = 36\) orders.
  3. One order: \(0.2^3 \times 0.8^7 = 0.008 \times 0.2097 \approx 0.001678\).
  4. Multiply: \(36 \times 0.001678 \approx 0.0604\). (On average it takes \(r/p = 15\) calls.)
P(X = 10) ≈ 0.060, about 1 in 17
Geometric · "more than"

Getting through on the phone

Each time you ring a busy helpline, you get through with chance 0.3, independently.

What's the chance you need more than 3 tries?

  1. Waiting for the first success is Geometric: Negative Binomial with \(r = 1\), \(p = 0.3\).
  2. "More than 3 tries" means the first 3 all fail, so there's no need to add up the endless tail.
  3. \(P(X \ge 4) = 0.7^3 = 0.343\).
P(X ≥ 4) = 0.343, about 1 in 3
Sports · "at most"

Winning a best-of-7 series

A team wins each game with chance 0.6, independently. A best-of-7 series goes to the first team with 4 wins.

What's the chance the team gets its 4 wins within 7 games?

  1. Count games until the 4th win: \(X \sim \text{NegBin}(4,\ 0.6)\). Winning the series means \(X \le 7\).
  2. \(P(4) = 0.6^4 = 0.1296\)
  3. \(P(5) = \binom{4}{3}\,0.6^4\,0.4 = 0.2074\), \(P(6) = \binom{5}{3}\,0.6^4\,0.4^2 = 0.2074\)
  4. \(P(7) = \binom{6}{3}\,0.6^4\,0.4^3 = 0.1659\)
  5. Add: \(0.1296 + 0.2074 + 0.2074 + 0.1659 \approx 0.710\).
P(X ≤ 7) ≈ 0.710
Poisson

Worked examples

Help desk · "none"

A completely quiet hour

A help desk gets on average 4 calls an hour, at random moments.

What's the chance of no calls at all in the next hour?

  1. Check: random events at a steady rate, counted in a fixed window. So \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(4)\).
  2. \(P(X = 0) = \frac{4^0 e^{-4}}{0!} = e^{-4}\).
  3. \(e^{-4} \approx 0.0183\).
P(X = 0) ≈ 0.018, about 1 hour in 55
Publishing · "at most"

Typos on a page

A draft has on average 2.5 typos per page.

What's the chance a page has at most 1 typo?

  1. \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(2.5)\). "At most 1" is \(F(1) = P(0) + P(1)\).
  2. \(P(0) = e^{-2.5} \approx 0.082\)
  3. \(P(1) = 2.5\,e^{-2.5} \approx 0.205\)
  4. Add: \(0.082 + 0.205 = 0.287\).
P(X ≤ 1) ≈ 0.287
Scaling λ · "at least"

A busy two-hour shift

Same help desk, 4 calls an hour on average. Your shift lasts 2 hours.

What's the chance you take at least 10 calls?

  1. λ belongs to the window: \(\lambda = 4 \times 2 = 8\) calls per shift.
  2. "At least 10" is an endless tail, so flip it: \(P(X \ge 10) = 1 - F(9)\).
  3. \(F(9) = P(0) + \dots + P(9) \approx 0.717\).
  4. \(1 - 0.717 = 0.283\).
P(X ≥ 10) ≈ 0.283
