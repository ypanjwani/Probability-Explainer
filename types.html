Types of Probability

Marginal, joint and conditional probability, read off a single table. Then the rules that connect them.

Stage 0

Marginal Probability

One question at a time, ignoring the other

All through this page we'll use one small survey. 200 people were asked two questions: coffee or tea? and morning person or night person? Every person lands in exactly one of four boxes, so the answers fit in a two-way table.

Quick notation: an event is something that can happen, written with a capital letter, such as \(A\) = "drinks coffee". \(P(A)\) is its probability: the share of people for whom it's true. \(A \cap B\) means "A and B", and \(A^c\) means "not A".

The simplest question asks about one variable and ignores the other: "what share drink coffee?" That's a marginal probability.

DefinitionThe marginal probability of an event is the probability that it happens, taken on its own, whatever the other variables turn out to be. For a random variable \(X\) the full list of these, \(P(X = x)\) for every value \(x\), is its marginal distribution.
Example: 80 coffee drinkers are morning people and 40 are night people. So 120 of the 200 drink coffee, and \(P(\text{Coffee}) = 120/200 = 0.60\). Whether they like mornings doesn't matter: both kinds get added in.

Why is it called "marginal"?

Before computers, a contingency table (a table that cross-counts two variables, like this one) was filled in by hand. The row and column totals were written in the margins of the page, to the right and along the bottom, and the probabilities you get from them kept the name. The word has nothing to do with "small" or "unimportant".

The name stuck in pictures too. When a scatter plot of two variables has a histogram of each one drawn along its top and side edges (the continuous card below), those edge plots are called marginal plots.

Unconditional: no "given" anywhere

\(P(\text{Coffee})\) has no bar \(\mid\) in it. It's the chance before you learn anything about the other variable, so it's also called the unconditional probability. Compare it with the conditionals from Stage 2, where you're told the body clock first:

Told "morning person"\(P(\text{Coffee} \mid \text{Morning}) = 80/110 \approx 0.73\)
Told "night person"\(P(\text{Coffee} \mid \text{Night}) = 40/90 \approx 0.44\)
Told nothing (marginal)\(P(\text{Coffee}) = 0.60\). It sits between the two, as their average weighted by group size: \(0.73 \times 0.55 + 0.44 \times 0.45 = 0.60\). Stage 4 turns this into a rule.
Careful: unconditional ≠ independent. A marginal doesn't depend on the other variable's value, because it adds up over all of them. That doesn't mean the two variables are unrelated. Here coffee and mornings clearly go together (0.73 vs 0.44). The marginal just ignores that link; it doesn't remove it. Checking whether two variables really are independent takes the joint table (Stage 3).

Why adding works

Step 1 · Split the eventEvery coffee drinker is either a morning person or a night person, never both and never neither. So "Coffee" breaks into two pieces that don't overlap: \(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Morning}\) and \(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Night}\).
Step 2 · Non-overlapping pieces addWhen pieces don't overlap, their probabilities just add up. $$P(\text{Coffee}) = P(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Morning}) + P(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Night})$$
Step 3 · Check\(\frac{80}{200} + \frac{40}{200} = 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.60\). That matches the row total, 120/200.
Step 4 · The name for thisAdding over every value of a variable you don't care about is called marginalising it out (or "summing it out"). With more variables you sum out all of them: \(P(x) = \sum_y \sum_z P(x, y, z)\).

Continuous variables: integrate instead of add

Height and weight don't come in a few boxes; they can take any value. Their joint distribution is a joint density \(f_{X,Y}(x, y)\), a cloud rather than a table. The idea is the same: to get the marginal of \(X\), collect everything in a thin column at \(x\), over every possible \(y\). With infinitely many values of \(y\), "add them all" becomes an integral.

Marginal probability in machine learning

Class balance and baselinesThe marginal distribution of the label, \(P(y)\), is the first thing to check. If 99% of transactions are not fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" scores 99% accuracy. That's why imbalanced data needs other metrics, reweighting or stratified splits.
Priors in naive BayesA naive Bayes classifier starts from \(P(\text{class})\), the marginal share of each class in the training data. The normaliser \(P(x) = \sum_y P(x \mid y)\,P(y)\) is the marginal of the features.
Latent-variable modelsGaussian mixtures, topic models and VAEs have hidden variables \(z\). They're trained to make the data likely, which means the marginal \(p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\): the hidden part is integrated out.
Bayesian model comparisonThe marginal likelihood (or evidence), \(p(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta\), averages how well a model fits over all its parameter settings. Comparing it between models is how Bayesian methods pick one.
Data drift monitoringProduction systems compare each feature's marginal distribution in live data against training, using a histogram per feature and tests like PSI or Kolmogorov–Smirnov. The catch, from the card above: marginals can stay the same while the relationship between features changes.
Probabilistic graphical modelsIn a Bayesian network, "how likely is this disease, given the symptoms?" is answered by summing out every other node. Algorithms like belief propagation exist to do that marginalisation efficiently.

Common mistakes

Dividing by the wrong totalA marginal divides by the grand total \(N\). Dividing a cell by a row total gives a conditional instead.
Reading a link from the margins"60% drink coffee and 55% are morning people" says nothing about how many do both. That number is anywhere from 30 to 110 people.
Unconditional = independentA marginal ignores the other variable; it doesn't show the variables are unrelated.
Summing pieces that overlapThe sum works because the pieces (Morning, Night) don't overlap and cover everyone. Overlapping pieces would be counted twice.

Stage 1

Joint Probability

Two things true at the same time

Marginals answer one question at a time. A joint probability asks about both at once: "what share drink coffee and are morning people?"

DefinitionThe joint probability of two events is the probability that they both happen together, written \(P(A \cap B)\). For two random variables, \(P(X = x,\, Y = y)\) is the chance that \(X\) takes the value \(x\) and \(Y\) takes the value \(y\) at the same time. The full list of these for every pair \((x, y)\) is the joint distribution.

In a two-way table, a joint probability is a single cell divided by everyone. In a Venn diagram it's the overlap of the two circles. You'll see it written several ways, all meaning the same thing: \(P(A \cap B)\), \(P(A, B)\), \(P(AB)\), or "\(P(A \text{ and } B)\)".

Example: 80 of the 200 are coffee-drinking morning people, so \(P(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Morning}) = 80/200 = 0.40\). The other three cells: coffee and night 0.20, tea and morning 0.15, tea and night 0.25. Together: 1.00.

Order doesn't matter"Coffee and morning" is the same group as "morning and coffee": \(P(A \cap B) = P(B \cap A)\). This symmetry is what Bayes' theorem is built on.

How big can the overlap be?

If you only know the marginals, 60% coffee and 55% morning, the joint isn't fixed. But it can't be just anything:

Upper bound · the overlap fits inside both circlesCoffee-and-morning people are a subset of coffee people and of morning people. So \(P(A \cap B) \le \min\{0.60,\, 0.55\} = 0.55\): at most 110 people.
Lower bound · the circles must overlap if they're big enough60% + 55% = 115%, more than everyone. At least 15% of people have to be in both: \(P(A \cap B) \ge 0.60 + 0.55 - 1 = 0.15\), or 30 people. If the two marginals add to less than 1, the lower bound is just 0.
Where the real answer comes fromAnything from 0.15 to 0.55 is possible. To pin it down you need either the table itself, or how the two variables are related. That's the next section.

Independent or dependent: two ways to build a joint

When you don't have the full table, the joint is built from smaller pieces. There's one rule that always works, and a shortcut that only works when the variables are independent (knowing one tells you nothing about the other, Stage 3).

Always true (dependent or not)Take the chance of A, then the chance of B once A has happened: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\). This is the multiplication rule from Stage 2.
Only if independentIf A doesn't change the chance of B, then \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\), and the rule shrinks to \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\): just multiply the marginals.

Continuous variables: probability is volume

For continuous variables like height and weight, the joint distribution is a joint density \(f_{X,Y}(x, y)\): a hill over the \((x, y)\) plane, highest where the points are thickest. A probability is now the volume under the hill over a region, \(\iint_R f_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dx\,dy\). In the cloud below, that volume is simply the share of points inside the box.

Two consequences: the chance of hitting one exact pair, say exactly 170.000… cm and 70.000… kg, is 0 (a single point has no volume), so you always ask about a region. And if \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, the hill is a product: \(f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\).

The joint CDF is a corner box"Make it a CDF box" stretches the box down and left forever. Its volume is the joint CDF, \(F(x, y) = P(X \le x,\, Y \le y)\).

More than two events: the chain rule

The multiplication rule keeps going. Each new event is multiplied in, conditioned on everything before it:

Step 1 · First cardDraw three cards without putting any back. The first is a heart: \(P(H_1) = 13/52\).
Step 2 · Second card, given the first12 hearts are left among 51 cards: \(P(H_2 \mid H_1) = 12/51\).
Step 3 · Third card, given both\(P(H_3 \mid H_1, H_2) = 11/50\). Multiply all three: $$P(H_1 \cap H_2 \cap H_3) = \frac{13}{52} \cdot \frac{12}{51} \cdot \frac{11}{50} \approx 0.0129$$
CheckIf the draws were independent (each card put back), it would be \((1/4)^3 \approx 0.0156\). Not putting cards back makes later hearts rarer.

Joint probability in machine learning

The confusion matrixRows = true class, columns = predicted class. Divide every cell by the total and you have the joint distribution of (truth, prediction). Precision and recall are conditionals read from it.
Generative vs discriminative modelsA generative model (naive Bayes, Gaussian discriminant analysis) learns the whole joint \(p(x, y)\) and can create new examples. A discriminative model (logistic regression, most neural classifiers) learns only \(p(y \mid x)\).
Language models use the chain ruleThe probability of a sentence is a joint over its words, built word by word: \(p(w_1, \dots, w_n) = \prod_i p(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})\). GPT-style models learn each factor.
Market-basket analysisIn association rules, the support of {bread, butter} is the joint probability that a basket contains both. Rules like "bread → butter" are then ranked by conditionals.
Co-occurrence and embeddingsWord embeddings like GloVe start from how often two words appear together: a joint probability table over pairs of words.
Feature interactionsTwo features can each look useless on their own (flat marginals) yet predict perfectly together, like XOR. Only the joint shows it.

Common mistakes

Multiplying dependent events\(P(A)\,P(B)\) is only right for independent events. For the survey it gives 0.33 instead of 0.40.
Joint vs conditionalA joint divides a cell by everyone (80/200). Dividing by a row total (80/120) gives a conditional instead.
"And" vs "or""Coffee and morning" is the overlap (0.40). "Coffee or morning" is everything in either circle, a bigger group (0.75).
Point probabilities for continuous variables\(f_{X,Y}(170, 70)\) is a height on the hill, not a probability; for tightly packed data it can even be bigger than 1. Only volumes over regions are probabilities.

Stage 2

Conditional Probability

Zoom in on a row, then count again

A conditional probability answers a question after you've been told something: "given this person drinks coffee, how likely are they a morning person?" Written \(P(\text{Morning} \mid \text{Coffee})\). The bar \(\mid\) is read "given".

"Given coffee" means ignore everyone else. The coffee row becomes the whole world, and its total, not 200, goes on the bottom of the fraction.

Example: of the 120 coffee drinkers, 80 are morning people: \(P(\text{Morning} \mid \text{Coffee}) = 80/120 \approx 0.67\). Among tea drinkers it's only \(30/80 = 0.375\). Compare the joint, \(P(\text{Coffee} \cap \text{Morning}) = 80/200 = 0.40\): same top, different bottom.

"Given" changes the bottomEverything outside the given row or column fades. Its total (blue) is the new denominator, and the percentages inside it now add to 100%.

The tree multipliesEach first branch is a marginal and each second branch is a conditional. Multiplying along a path gives the joint at its leaf: \(0.60 \times 0.67 = 0.40\).

Where the formula comes from

Step 1 · Zoom in on BKeep only the B row, the 120 coffee drinkers. They are the new "everyone".
Step 2 · Count A inside BAmong them, 80 are also morning people. Those 80 are the cell \(A \cap B\).
Step 3 · Counts → probabilitiesDivide the top and bottom by \(N = 200\). The answer doesn't change, but now both pieces are probabilities you already know: $$\frac{80}{120} = \frac{80/200}{120/200} = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$
Step 4 · Check\(0.40 / 0.60 \approx 0.667\), the same as \(80/120\). Turned around, \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\): the multiplication rule the tree uses.
Conditional probability
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$
Multiplication rule
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$$
Inside one given world
$$\sum_{a} P(A = a \mid B) = 1$$

Stage 3

Independence

When knowing one tells you nothing about the other

Two events are independent if learning one doesn't change the chance of the other: \(P(\text{Morning} \mid \text{Coffee}) = P(\text{Morning})\). In the survey that's false: 67% of coffee drinkers are morning people, against 55% overall. Drink and body clock are linked (dependent).

There's an equivalent test that only uses the table: the joint equals the product of the marginals, \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\). That's what lets you multiply probabilities of independent things, like two coin flips.

Example: if drink and body clock were independent, the coffee-morning cell would be \(0.60 \times 0.55 = 0.33\), or 66 people. The survey has 80. More coffee drinkers are morning people than independence would predict.

Stage 4

Law of Total Probability

Rebuild a marginal from its conditionals

Sometimes you don't have the whole table. You only know how each group behaves (conditionals) and how big each group is (marginals). The law of total probability puts them back together: split the world into groups, find the chance inside each group, and take an average weighted by group size.

It's the tree from Stage 2 read in the other direction: add up every leaf where the event happens.

Example: a factory has three machines. A makes 50% of the parts (2% defective), B makes 30% (3% defective), C makes 20% (5% defective). Overall: \(0.50 \times 0.02 + 0.30 \times 0.03 + 0.20 \times 0.05 = 0.029\). That's 2.9%, not the plain average of the three rates (3.3%).

Putting It Together

Simpson's paradox, and a map of the rules

Mixing up marginal and conditional probabilities can flip a conclusion. In a well-known 1986 study of kidney-stone treatments, treatment A did better than B for small stones and also for large stones. Yet B looked better overall. This is Simpson's paradox.

Switch between split and combined
Show:
Treatment ATreatment B
Who got which cases

What went wrong

Step 1 · The conditionals agreeSmall stones: A 81/87 = 93%, B 234/270 = 87%. Large stones: A 192/263 = 73%, B 55/80 = 69%. A wins both.
Step 2 · The mix differsDoctors gave A to the hard cases: 263 of A's 350 patients had large stones, against 80 of B's 350.
Step 3 · The overall rate is a weighted averageBy the law of total probability (Stage 4), each overall rate averages the two conditionals, weighted by that treatment's own mix: $$P(\text{success} \mid A) = 0.93 \cdot \tfrac{87}{350} + 0.73 \cdot \tfrac{263}{350} = 0.78$$ $$P(\text{success} \mid B) = 0.87 \cdot \tfrac{270}{350} + 0.69 \cdot \tfrac{80}{350} = 0.83$$ A's average leans on its hard cases and B's on its easy ones, so B comes out ahead.
LessonWhen the groups being compared have different mixes, compare like with like: use the conditionals.

Marginal · from counts

How many emails have a link?

A spam filter's training set has 400 emails. 60 are spam with a link, 20 are spam without one, 90 are normal emails with a link and 230 are normal emails without one.

What is P(has a link)? And P(spam)?

  1. Add down the "Has a link" column, over both kinds of email: \(60 + 90 = 150\).
  2. \(P(\text{Link}) = 150/400 = 0.375\), so \(P(\text{No link}) = 1 - 0.375 = 0.625\).

Marginal · machine learning

The 99.5% accurate fraud model

10,000 card transactions: 30 fraud abroad, 20 fraud at home, 970 genuine abroad, 8,980 genuine at home.

What is the marginal P(fraud)? How accurate is a model that never flags anything?

  1. Add along the Fraud row: \(30 + 20 = 50\), so \(P(\text{Fraud}) = 50/10{,}000 = 0.005\).
  2. Always answering "genuine" is right on the other 9,950: accuracy \(= 1 - 0.005 = 0.995\).

Runners who also lift

A gym has 400 members. 70% run and 50% lift weights.

Without asking anyone, what share could do both? Then a survey finds 120 who do both: does it fit?

  1. Upper bound: the "both" group sits inside the smaller group, so \(P(\text{Run} \cap \text{Lift}) \le \min\{0.70, 0.50\} = 0.50\).

Joint · machine learning

A confusion matrix is a joint table

A cat-vs-dog classifier is tested on 200 images. True cats: 90 called cat, 10 called dog. True dogs: 30 called cat, 70 called dog.

What is P(true cat ∩ called dog)? What is the accuracy?

  1. Each cell divided by all 200 is a joint probability of (truth, prediction). \(P(\text{Cat} \cap \text{Said dog}) = 10/200 = 0.05\).
  2. The model is right when truth and prediction match: the two diagonal cells. Accuracy \(= P(\text{Cat} \cap \text{Said cat}) + P(\text{Dog} \cap \text{Said dog}) = 0.45 + 0.35 = 0.80\).
  3. Check: all four joints add to 1 (\(0.45 + 0.05 + 0.15 + 0.35\)). The errors are \(0.05 + 0.15 = 0.20\), and most of them are dogs called cats.
P(cat ∩ said dog) = 0.05 · accuracy = 0.80

Tall and heavy

In the height × weight card, height has mean 170 cm (SD 10) and weight has mean 70 kg (SD 12), with correlation 0.6.

What's the chance an adult is both taller than 180 cm and heavier than 82 kg?

  1. Both cut-offs are 1 SD above the mean, so each marginal is \(P(Z > 1) \approx 0.159\).

Online vs in-person classes

240 students: 120 took a course online (72 passed, 48 failed) and 120 took it in person (96 passed, 24 failed).

What is P(passed | online)? How does it compare with P(passed | in person) and the overall pass rate?

  1. "Given online" means keep only the 120 online students: \(P(\text{Passed} \mid \text{Online}) = 72/120 = 0.60\).

Conditional · multiplication rule

Add to cart, then buy

A shop has 1,000 visitors. 30% add something to their cart, and 40% of those go on to buy. Nobody buys without adding to the cart first.

What share of all visitors buy? And what is P(added to cart | bought)?

  1. Multiply along the tree's branch: \(P(\text{Cart} \cap \text{Bought}) = P(\text{Cart})\,P(\text{Bought} \mid \text{Cart}) = 0.30 \times 0.40 = 0.12\), or 120 visitors.
  2. The other branch: \(P(\text{Bought} \mid \text{No cart}) = 0\), so it adds nothing. Overall, 12% of visitors buy.
  3. Flip it: \(P(\text{Cart} \mid \text{Bought}) = 120/120 = 1\). Every buyer added to the cart, though only 40% of cart-adders buy.
P(bought) = 0.12 · P(cart | bought) = 1
Conditional · shrinking the world

The two-child puzzle

A family has two children. Each is a boy or a girl with equal chance, so the four orders BB, BG, GB, GG are equally likely. You learn that at least one child is a girl.

What is the chance both children are girls?

  1. The tempting answer is 1/2. But "given at least one girl" only rules out BB.
  2. Three equally likely families are left: BG, GB, GG. Only one of them has two girls.
  3. \(P(\text{Both girls} \mid \text{Has a girl}) = \frac{1/4}{3/4} = \frac13\). The condition shrank the world from 4 cases to 3, not to 2.
1/3, not 1/2
Conditional · medical AI

Recall vs precision

An AI model screens 1,000 scans. 50 patients have the disease; the model flags 45 of them. Of the 950 healthy patients it also flags 95.

Find P(flagged | disease) and P(disease | flagged).

  1. Zoom into the 50 with the disease: \(P(\text{Flagged} \mid \text{Disease}) = 45/50 = 0.90\). In ML this is the recall.
  2. Zoom into the \(45 + 95 = 140\) flagged scans: \(P(\text{Disease} \mid \text{Flagged}) = 45/140 \approx 0.321\). This is the precision.
  3. Same 45 on top, very different bottoms. "Catches 90% of cases" does not mean "a flag is 90% sure": only about 1 flag in 3 is real, because healthy patients far outnumber sick ones.
Recall 0.90 · precision ≈ 0.32
Stage 3 · Worked examples

Independence

Independence · two dice

Does an even first die change the sum?

Roll two fair dice; all 36 ordered outcomes are equally likely. A = "the first die is even", B = "the sum is 7".

Are A and B independent?

  1. \(P(A) = 18/36 = \tfrac12\). Sums of 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1), so \(P(B) = 6/36 = \tfrac16\).
  2. Both: (2,5), (4,3), (6,1), so \(P(A \cap B) = 3/36 = \tfrac{1}{12}\).
  3. \(P(A)\,P(B) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac16 = \tfrac{1}{12}\). They match: independent. Whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second makes 7.
  4. Swap in "sum is 8" and it breaks: the first die is even in 3 of the 5 ways to make 8 [(2,6), (4,4), (6,2)], so the joint is 3/36, but \(\tfrac12 \times \tfrac{5}{36} = 2.5/36\). A first die of 1 can never reach 8.
Independent for a sum of 7, dependent for a sum of 8

Law of total probability

Total probability · factory

The faulty new machine

A factory adds a new machine C. A and B each make 40% of parts, with defect rates 1% and 2%. C makes the other 20%, with a defect rate of 9%.

What share of all parts is defective, and what share of the defects come from C?

  1. \(P(D) = 0.40(0.01) + 0.40(0.02) + 0.20(0.09) = 0.004 + 0.008 + 0.018 = 0.030\).
  2. The plain average of the rates, \((1 + 2 + 9)/3 = 4\%\), is wrong: it gives C as much weight as A and B, though C makes half as many parts as either.
  3. Flip it with Bayes: \(P(C \mid D) = 0.018 / 0.030 = 0.60\). C makes a fifth of the parts but 60% of the defects.
P(D) = 3.0% · P(C | D) = 60%
