Expected Value & Variance

Two numbers that sum up any random quantity: where it balances, and how far it strays. Plus the rules that make them so useful, and why the average alone can mislead you.

Stage 0

Expected Value

The long-run average, and the balance point

A raffle ticket costs $2. One ticket in 100 wins $150. Is it worth buying? You'll either lose $2 or gain $148, never anything in between. But if you bought tickets over and over, your average result per ticket would settle on one number. That number is the expected value.

To find it, weight every outcome by its chance and add up: \(0.99 \times (-2) + 0.01 \times 148 = -0.50\). On average you lose 50 cents per ticket. It's the same "mean" as on the Discrete distributions page, written \(E[X]\) or \(\mu\).

DefinitionThe expected value of a random variable \(X\) is the probability-weighted average of its values: \(E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X = x)\). It's where the PMF balances, and where the average of many independent repeats settles.
It needn't be a possible valueA die never shows 3.5, and a raffle never pays −$0.50. The expected value is an average, not a prediction for one try.
Rare big outcomes matterA 1-in-100 prize of $148 adds $1.48 to the average. Small chances times large amounts can dominate.
Example: roulette. A $1 bet on red wins $1 with chance 18/38 and loses $1 with chance 20/38. \(E = \frac{18}{38} - \frac{20}{38} = -\frac{2}{38} \approx -0.053\): the casino keeps about 5.3 cents of every dollar bet, in the long run.

Stage 1

Linearity of Expectation

Expected values pass straight through sums and scaling

Expected values obey two rules so simple they feel like cheating. Scale and shift: if every outcome is doubled and then $5 is subtracted, the average is doubled and then $5 is subtracted. Add: the expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values. That second rule holds even when the parts depend on each other.

Scale and shift\(E[aX + b] = a\,E[X] + b\). Pay $2 per pip on a die and charge $5: \(E = 2 \times 3.5 - 5 = 2\).
Sums\(E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]\), always. Ten dice total \(10 \times 3.5 = 35\) on average.
Not for every function\(E[X^2] \ne (E[X])^2\) and \(E[1/X] \ne 1/E[X]\) in general. Linearity is only for "times a constant, plus a constant".
Example: how many sixes in 12 rolls, on average? Instead of working out the whole distribution, score each roll 1 if it's a six (chance 1/6) and 0 otherwise. Each score averages \(1/6\), and there are 12 of them: \(12 \times \frac{1}{6} = 2\).

Reading the graph

a stretches, b slidesMultiplying by \(a\) stretches the spacing between outcomes (and the spread) by \(|a|\). Adding \(b\) slides everything, mean included, without changing the shape.
Sums: the mean grows like nThe total of \(n\) dice is centred on \(3.5n\). The shape changes completely (it turns into a bell), but the mean doesn't care.
Counting with 0/1 scoresSixes in \(n\) rolls is a sum of \(n\) yes/no scores, each with mean \(1/6\). The count's mean is \(n/6\) without any binomial algebra.

The indicator trick: expected counts without the distribution

Step 1 · Write the count as a sum of 0/1 scores"How many of these things happen?" = \(I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_m\), where \(I_j\) is 1 if thing \(j\) happens and 0 if not.
Step 2 · Each score's mean is its probability\(E[I_j] = 1 \cdot P(\text{happens}) + 0 \cdot P(\text{doesn't}) = P(\text{thing } j \text{ happens})\).
Step 3 · Add, ignoring any dependenceLinearity doesn't need independence, so \(E[\text{count}] = \sum_j P(\text{thing } j)\), even when the things are tangled together.
Step 4 · Check: shared birthdays30 people have \(\binom{30}{2} = 435\) pairs, and each pair shares a birthday with chance \(1/365\). Expected number of matching pairs: \(435/365 \approx 1.19\). (The pairs aren't independent; it doesn't matter.)
Step 5 · Check: the hat-check problem\(n\) people get their hats back at random. Each gets their own with chance \(1/n\), so the expected number of correct hats is \(n \times \frac{1}{n} = 1\), for 5 people or 5 million.

Stage 2

Variance & Standard Deviation

How far, on average, outcomes land from the mean

Two games can have the same expected value and feel completely different. A sure $3.50 and a die roll both average 3.5. We need a second number for spread.

The variance takes each outcome's distance from the mean, squares it (so left and right misses both count, and big misses count extra), and averages those squares with the usual probability weights. Its square root, the standard deviation \(\sigma\), is back in the original units.

Definition\(\text{Var}(X) = E\bigl[(X - \mu)^2\bigr] = \sum_x (x - \mu)^2 P(X = x)\), where \(\mu = E[X]\). The standard deviation is \(\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\).
Why square?Plain distances \(x - \mu\) average to exactly 0: the misses on each side cancel. Squaring makes every miss count as positive.
Why take the root?Variance is in squared units ("dollars²"). \(\sigma\) is in dollars again, so you can say "typically about $1.71 from the mean".
Example: a fair die. Distances from 3.5 are ±2.5, ±1.5, ±0.5. Their squares are 6.25, 2.25, 0.25 (each twice). Average: \(\frac{2(6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25)}{6} = \frac{17.5}{6} \approx 2.92\). So \(\sigma = \sqrt{2.92} \approx 1.71\).

A faster way to compute it

Step 3 · Simplify$$\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \bigl(E[X]\bigr)^2$$ "the mean of the square, minus the square of the mean".
Step 4 · CheckFair die: \(E[X^2] = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17\). Then \(15.17 - 3.5^2 = 15.17 - 12.25 = 2.92\). Same answer.

Stage 3

Variance of Sums & Covariance

Means always add; spreads add only when the parts are independent

Scaling works almost like the mean, with one twist: \(\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\,\text{Var}(X)\). Shifting by \(b\) moves everything together, so the spread doesn't change; scaling by \(a\) scales distances by \(|a|\) and squared distances by \(a^2\).

Sums are where it gets interesting. Add two dice and the mean is always 7. But the spread of the total depends on how the dice are related. If they move together, their misses pile up. If they move in opposite directions, their misses cancel. The amount they move together is the covariance.

Definition\(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = E\bigl[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\bigr]\): positive when \(X\) and \(Y\) tend to be above (or below) their means together, negative when one tends to be high while the other is low, and 0 for independent variables.
Example: two independent dice: \(\text{Var} = 2.92 + 2.92 = 5.83\), so the total is \(7 \pm 2.42\). If the "second die" just copies the first, the total is \(2X\): variance \(4 \times 2.92 = 11.67\). If the second die always shows \(7 - X\), the total is always exactly 7: variance 0.

The mean never moves off 7: linearity doesn't care about dependence. The variance swings from 0 to 11.67, and the covariance term is what accounts for the whole difference.

Reading the graph

Independent: variances addCov = 0, so \(\text{Var}(X + Y) = 2.92 + 2.92 = 5.83\). The familiar triangle of two dice.
Moving together widensPositive covariance adds extra spread. A full copy doubles the miss every time: the totals are only 2, 4, …, 12.
Moving against narrowsNegative covariance cancels misses. \(Y = 7 - X\) cancels them perfectly. That's the idea behind hedging and diversification.

Why independent variances add, and averages steady down

Step 1 · Expand the squared miss of the sum\((X + Y - \mu_X - \mu_Y)^2 = (X - \mu_X)^2 + (Y - \mu_Y)^2 + 2(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\).
Step 2 · Take expected values$$\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y)$$
Step 3 · Independent: the cross term is 0For independent variables the misses are equally likely to agree or disagree in sign, so \(\text{Cov} = 0\) and variances simply add. That's also why \(\text{Var}(X - Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\): subtracting doesn't cancel independent noise.
Step 4 · The average of n independent copiesThe sum has variance \(n\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n\) divides variance by \(n^2\): \(\text{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n\), so \(\text{SD}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\). This is why insurers pool thousands of policies.
Step 5 · Check"Half the time a copy": \(\text{Cov} = 0.5 \times 2.92 = 1.46\). Then \(\text{Var} = 5.83 + 2 \times 1.46 = 8.75\). Pick that option above: the card reads 8.75.

Stage 4

Expected Value vs Risk

Same average, very different rides

Expected value tells you what happens on average over many tries. But you might only get a few tries. Three bets below all cost $45 and all have expected value +$5 per play. They differ only in variance, and that decides how likely you are to end up behind after a given number of plays.

A · Sure thingAlways pays $50. Net +$5, \(\sigma = 0\).
B · Coin flip50% chance of $100, else $0. Net +$55 or −$45: \(\sigma = 50\).
C · Long shot10% chance of $500, else $0. Net +$455 or −$45: \(\sigma = 150\).
Example: play the long shot once and you lose 90% of the time. Play it 100 times and you still finish behind about 32% of the time, despite a positive expected value. The coin flip, with a third of the spread, is behind only 14% of the time after 100 plays.

Stage 5

Continuous Random Variables

Same ideas, with integrals instead of sums

A waiting time or a height can take any value in a range, so there's no list of outcomes to add up. Instead there's a density curve \(f(x)\) (see the Continuous distributions page). The recipe is the same: weight each value by how likely it is, and add. With a continuum of values, "add" becomes an integral: the area under \(x\,f(x)\).

Example: a bus comes at a random moment in the next 10 minutes (uniform). Your expected wait is 5 minutes, the balance point of a flat block. The SD is \(10/\sqrt{12} \approx 2.89\) minutes.

Putting It Together

Two numbers, a handful of rules

Expected value tells you where a random quantity balances; variance tells you how far it strays. Expected values pass straight through sums and scaling. Variances add for independent parts, and that is why averages settle and pooled risks shrink. Click any box to see how the ideas connect.

Common mistakes

Expecting the expected valueOne play of the long shot ends at −$45 90% of the time. EV describes the long run, not the next try.
\(E[X^2] = (E[X])^2\)Only when there's no spread. The gap between them is the variance.
Adding standard deviationsVariances add (for independent parts); SDs don't. Two dice: \(\sigma = \sqrt{2.92 + 2.92} = 2.42\), not \(1.71 + 1.71 = 3.42\).
Subtracting to cancel noise\(\text{Var}(X - Y)\) is \(\text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\) for independent \(X, Y\). Differences are noisier, not quieter.

Expected value

Worked examples

Raffle · is it worth it?

A $2 ticket, a $150 prize

Tickets cost $2. One in 100 wins $150.

What's the expected net result per ticket?

  1. Outcomes: win (net +$148) with chance 0.01, lose (net −$2) with chance 0.99.
  2. \(E = 0.01 \times 148 + 0.99 \times (-2) = 1.48 - 1.98\).
  3. \(E = -0.50\). The fair price would be $1.50 (the prize × its chance).
E = −$0.50 per ticket
Casino · the house edge

1,000 bets on red

You bet $1 on red, 1,000 times. Red comes up with chance 18/38.

How much do you expect to lose in total?

  1. One bet: \(E = \frac{18}{38}(+1) + \frac{20}{38}(-1) = -\frac{2}{38} \approx -0.0526\).
  2. 1,000 bets: by linearity, \(1000 \times (-0.0526) \approx -52.6\).
  3. Any single bet is close to a coin flip. The loss only shows up in the long run.
Expected loss ≈ $52.60
Insurance · the other side

An insurer's profit per policy

A policy costs $300 a year. With chance 0.02 the insurer pays a $10,000 claim.

What's the insurer's expected profit per policy?

  1. No claim: +$300 (chance 0.98). Claim: \(300 - 10{,}000 = -9{,}700\) (chance 0.02).
  2. \(E = 0.98 \times 300 + 0.02 \times (-9700) = 294 - 194 = 100\).
  3. But one policy is very risky (σ = $1,400). Insurers sell thousands, so the average settles (Stage 3).
E = +$100 per policy
Linearity

Worked examples

Scale and shift

$2 per pip, $5 to play

A game pays $2 for every pip on one die roll and costs $5.

What's your expected profit?

  1. Profit \(= 2X - 5\), with \(E[X] = 3.5\).
  2. \(E[2X - 5] = 2 \times 3.5 - 5 = 2\).
  3. No need to list the six profits (−3, −1, 1, 3, 5, 7) and average them, though it gives the same 2.
E = +$2
Sums · no distribution needed

The total of ten dice

You roll ten dice and add them.

What's the expected total?

  1. Total \(= X_1 + \dots + X_{10}\).
  2. Linearity: \(E = E[X_1] + \dots + E[X_{10}] = 10 \times 3.5 = 35\).
  3. The full distribution of the total has 51 possible values; the mean needed none of them.
E = 35
Indicators · counting

Sixes in twelve rolls

You roll a die 12 times.

How many sixes do you expect?

  1. Let \(I_j = 1\) if roll \(j\) is a six. Count \(= I_1 + \dots + I_{12}\).
  2. \(E[I_j] = P(\text{six}) = \frac{1}{6}\).
  3. \(E[\text{count}] = 12 \times \frac{1}{6} = 2\). (It's the Binomial mean \(np\), derived in one line.)
E = 2 sixes

Two methods · same answer

A fair die's variance

One fair die.

Find the variance by the definition and by the shortcut.

Maximum spread

A die that shows only 1 or 6

A trick die has three 1s and three 6s.

What are its mean and variance?

  1. Mean: \(0.5 \times 1 + 0.5 \times 6 = 3.5\), the same as a fair die.
  2. Every outcome is 2.5 from the mean: \(\text{Var} = 2.5^2 = 6.25\).
  3. \(\sigma = 2.5\). No die with faces 1 to 6 can be more spread out.
μ = 3.5, Var = 6.25, σ = 2.5
Loaded · the mean moves

A die loaded on 6

A die shows 6 half the time; faces 1 to 5 get 0.1 each.

What are the mean and variance?

  1. Mean: \(0.1(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) + 0.5 \times 6 = 1.5 + 3 = 4.5\).
  2. \(E[X^2] = 0.1(1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25) + 0.5 \times 36 = 5.5 + 18 = 23.5\).
  3. \(\text{Var} = 23.5 - 4.5^2 = 23.5 - 20.25 = 3.25\), so \(\sigma \approx 1.80\).
μ = 4.5, Var = 3.25

Perfectly linked · doubling

A copied die

The second "die" always shows the same face as the first, so the total is \(2X\).

What's the variance of the total?

  1. By scaling: \(\text{Var}(2X) = 4 \times 2.917 = 11.67\).
  2. By the sum rule: \(\text{Cov}(X, X) = \text{Var}(X)\), so \(2.917 + 2.917 + 2 \times 2.917 = 11.67\). ✓
  3. \(\sigma = 3.42\): twice one die's spread.
Var = 11.67, σ ≈ 3.42
Hedge · cancelling

The opposite face

The second die shows \(7 - X\): the face on the bottom of the first.

What's the variance of the total?

  1. The total is \(X + 7 - X = 7\), every time.
  2. Sum rule: \(\text{Cov}(X, 7 - X) = -\text{Var}(X)\), so \(2.917 + 2.917 - 2 \times 2.917 = 0\). ✓
  3. That's a perfect hedge: two risky pieces that cancel exactly.
Var = 0
Expected value vs risk

Worked examples

One play · EV isn't a forecast

Playing the long shot once

Bet C: pay $45, win $500 with chance 0.1.

What are the EV, the SD, and the chance of losing?

  1. EV: \(0.1 \times 455 + 0.9 \times (-45) = 45.5 - 40.5 = +5\).
  2. SD: \(500\sqrt{0.1 \times 0.9} = 500 \times 0.3 = 150\).
  3. You lose $45 with chance 0.9, despite the positive EV.
EV +$5, σ $150, P(behind) = 0.90
Repetition · the coin flip

100 coin-flip bets

Bet B, played 100 times.

How likely are you to finish behind?

  1. You're behind if total winnings \(100W < 4500\): fewer than 45 wins out of 100.
  2. Exact Binomial(100, 0.5): \(P(W \le 44) \approx 0.136\).
  3. Rough check: SD of the average is \(50/\sqrt{100} = 5\), so 0 is one SD below +5: about 16% by the normal curve.
P(behind) ≈ 0.136
Repetition · the long shot

Still losing after 100 plays

Bet C, played 100 times.

How likely are you to finish behind?

  1. Behind if \(500W < 4500\): fewer than 9 wins out of 100.
  2. Exact Binomial(100, 0.1): \(P(W \le 8) \approx 0.321\).
  3. SD of the average: \(150/\sqrt{100} = 15\), three times the edge. It takes about 1,000 plays to bring P(behind) down to 0.13.
P(behind) ≈ 0.321

Worked examples

Uniform · the bus

Waiting up to 10 minutes

The bus arrives at a uniformly random time in the next 10 minutes.

What are the mean and SD of your wait?

  1. \(f(x) = \frac{1}{10}\) on 0 to 10. \(E[X] = \int_0^{10} \frac{x}{10}\,dx = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{10^2}{2} = 5\).
  2. \(\text{Var} = \frac{(10 - 0)^2}{12} \approx 8.33\).
  3. \(\sigma = 10/\sqrt{12} \approx 2.89\) minutes.
μ = 5 min, σ ≈ 2.89 min
Exponential · mean = SD

A call-centre wait

Waits are exponential with mean 4 minutes.

What's the SD, and where does the mean sit compared with the peak?

  1. For an exponential, \(\sigma = \mu\): the SD is also 4 minutes.
  2. The density peaks at 0 (short waits are most likely), but the long tail pulls the balance point out to 4.
  3. About 63% of waits are shorter than the mean: \(1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632\).
μ = σ = 4 min
Triangle · a skewed estimate

Peak at 2, range 0 to 10

A project estimate: usually about 2 weeks, never under 0, at most 10. Model it as a triangle with peak \(c = 2\).

What are the mean and SD?

  1. \(\mu = (0 + 2 + 10)/3 = 4\) weeks: double the "most likely" value.
  2. \(\text{Var} = (100 + 4 - 20)/18 \approx 4.67\).
  3. \(\sigma \approx 2.16\) weeks. Planning for the peak (2 weeks) would routinely run late.
μ = 4 weeks, σ ≈ 2.16
