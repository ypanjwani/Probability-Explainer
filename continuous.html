Continuous Probability Distributions

When the answer can be any number in a range, bars melt into smooth curves and probability becomes area.

Stage 0

Foundations

From bars to a smooth curve

Discrete answers can be listed: 0, 1, 2, 3… Continuous answers can be any number in a range: a height of 172.418 cm, a wait of 4.07 minutes, a temperature of 21.6°. There are infinitely many possible values, so we can't give each one its own bar.

So what do we draw instead? Group the values into bins and shrink the bins:

Heights of adults, grouped into bins
Shrink the bars, then switch what the height means
one binthe limit: a smooth curve

Slide the width down with "probability" selected: every bar gets shorter, because a thinner slice holds less probability. Shrink it all the way and each single value holds zero probability.

Now pick "probability ÷ width". The bars stop shrinking and settle onto a smooth curve. That curve is the probability density function, or PDF, written \(f(x)\). Its height is a density: probability per unit of \(x\), like people per cm.

Probability = areaThe chance of landing between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area under \(f(x)\) between them.
One exact value has probability 0\(P(X = 170) = 0\): a line has no width, so it has no area. Only ranges have probability.
< or ≤ doesn't matterSince single points carry no probability, \(P(X \le b) = P(X < b)\).
Density can go above 1Only the total area must be 1. A tall, thin curve can easily reach a height of 2 or 5.

Press Squeeze to one point and the shaded area collapses to nothing: that's why \(P(X = x) = 0\) for any single value. Measure 1000 people and compare: the share who land between the handles matches the shaded area.

The CDF \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\) is the area to the left of \(x\). It climbs smoothly from 0 to 1, with no steps this time. Any interval's probability is just the rise of the CDF across it: \(P(a \le X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\). The PDF is the CDF's slope: steep where values are common, flat where they're rare.

Definitions
$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$ $$F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt, \qquad f(x) = F'(x)$$ $$\mu = \int x\,f(x)\,dx, \qquad \sigma^2 = \int (x-\mu)^2 f(x)\,dx$$
In words

∫ is "add up infinitely thin slices": the continuous version of Σ.

Rules: \(f(x) \ge 0\) everywhere, and the total area \(\int f(x)\,dx = 1\).

Mean and variance mean exactly what they did for discrete distributions: balance point and spread.

Stage 1

Continuous Uniform Distribution

Any value in a range, no favourites

Every value between \(a\) and \(b\) is equally likely, and nothing outside is possible. The PDF is a flat rectangle. Its width is \(b - a\), and since the area must be 1, its height has to be \(\frac{1}{b-a}\).

Use it when all of these hold:

1 · A fixed range [a, b]Values can't fall below \(a\) or above \(b\).
Breaks if: there's no hard limit, like adult heights.
2 · No favouritesEqual-width slices anywhere in the range are equally likely.
Breaks if: values bunch up in the middle (that's more Normal-shaped).
Example: a bus comes every 10 minutes and you arrive at a random moment. Your wait is Uniform on [0, 10].

Stage 2

Exponential Distribution

How long until the next event?

Events happen at random at a steady average rate \(\lambda\) per unit of time: calls to a help desk, customers at a counter, clicks on a site. Poisson counts how many happen in a window. The Exponential measures the other thing: the waiting time until the next one.

Use it when all of these hold:

1 · You measure a waiting timeA time (or distance) from now until the next event, so it's always ≥ 0.
Breaks if: you count events instead. That's Poisson.
2 · A constant rate λEvents are equally likely to happen at any moment.
Breaks if: the rate changes, like a rush hour.
3 · Independent eventsOne event doesn't make the next come sooner or later.
Breaks if: events come in bursts or clusters.
4 · No ageingThe time already waited doesn't change what's left (see "memoryless" below).
Breaks if: things wear out, like car parts.
Example: a help desk gets 2 calls per hour on average. The time until the next call is Exponential with \(\lambda = 2\), so the average wait is \(1/\lambda = 0.5\) hours.

It has no memory

Say you've already waited \(s\) minutes with nothing happening. How much longer will you wait? Exactly as long as a fresh start. The Exponential "forgets" the past: \(P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\). Slide \(s\) and watch the curve for the remaining wait (dashed). It's the original curve, just shifted.

The Poisson link

"No event in the next \(x\) time units" means the same thing two ways. Poisson says the count in that window is 0, which has probability \(e^{-\lambda x}\). Exponential says the wait is longer than \(x\), which also has probability \(e^{-\lambda x}\). The CDF follows straight from it: \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\).

PDF
$$f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \ge 0$$
CDF
$$F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$$
Summary
$$\mu = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$$

Mean and standard deviation are equal: both \(1/\lambda\).

What each piece means
\(\lambda\)
the rate: average number of events per unit of time
\(x\)
a length of waiting time
\(e^{-\lambda x}\)
the chance that nothing has happened yet after waiting \(x\)
\(\lambda \cdot\)
the rate at which the event arrives at that moment, given it hasn't yet

Stage 3

Normal Distribution

The bell curve

Add up many small, independent nudges, and the total lands on a bell shape. Height comes from many genes and nutrition, a measurement error from many tiny disturbances, an exam score from many questions. This is the Normal (or Gaussian) distribution. Two numbers describe it fully: its centre \(\mu\) and its spread \(\sigma\).

It's a good model when:

1 · Many small effects add upNo single cause dominates the result.
Breaks if: one big factor decides it, like income, which has a long right tail.
2 · Symmetric around the meanToo high is as likely as too low by the same amount.
Breaks if: the data is lopsided, like waiting times.
3 · No hard walls nearbyThe curve stretches forever both ways (though values beyond ±4σ are vanishingly rare).
Breaks if: values sit close to a limit like 0.
4 · Mostly near the middleAbout 68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ.
Breaks if: extreme values turn up far more often than that.
Example: adult heights with \(\mu = 170\) cm and \(\sigma = 8\) cm. Most people are 162–178 cm, and almost nobody is outside 146–194 cm.

z-scores: one bell to rule them all

Every Normal curve is the same shape, just shifted and stretched. Measure any value in "σ's away from the mean" and they all become the standard Normal, \(\mu = 0,\ \sigma = 1\):

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

A height of 186 cm with \(\mu = 170,\ \sigma = 8\) is \(z = 2\): two σ above average, taller than about 97.7% of people. The orange labels under the curve mark μ ± 1, 2, 3σ.

The CDF has no simple formula

The area under a bell curve can't be written with ordinary functions. So it gets its own name, \(\Phi(z)\), and is looked up in a z-table (or computed, as this page does). Every Normal question reduces to it: \(F(x) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\).

The empirical rule: 68 – 95 – 99.7

How much of a bell curve lies near its mean

The empirical rule (also called the 68–95–99.7 rule or the three-sigma rule) answers a simple question about any Normal distribution: what share of values land within a given number of standard deviations of the mean? The answer is always the same three numbers:

Within 1σ: about 68%Between \(\mu - \sigma\) and \(\mu + \sigma\) you find roughly 2 values out of 3.
Within 2σ: about 95%Between \(\mu - 2\sigma\) and \(\mu + 2\sigma\) you find 19 values out of 20.
Within 3σ: about 99.7%Between \(\mu - 3\sigma\) and \(\mu + 3\sigma\) you find almost everything. Only about 1 value in 370 is outside.

Written as probabilities, for \(X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma)\):

$$\begin{aligned} P(\mu - \sigma \le X \le \mu + \sigma) &\approx 0.6827 \quad (\text{about 68\%})\\ P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) &\approx 0.9545 \quad (\text{about 95\%})\\ P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) &\approx 0.9973 \quad (\text{about 99.7\%}) \end{aligned}$$

Two things make the rule useful. First, it doesn't depend on μ or σ: heights in centimetres, exam marks and machine errors in microns all follow the same percentages, measured in σ's. Second, it turns σ into something you can picture: "σ = 8 cm" means two-thirds of people are within 8 cm of average. It is called empirical because people noticed these shares in real measured data long before they were taught as theory. Try the widths and examples below, then see where the numbers come from.

Deriving the rule

Probability for a continuous variable is area under the PDF. So the empirical rule is just the area under the bell curve between \(\mu - k\sigma\) and \(\mu + k\sigma\), worked out for \(k = 1, 2, 3\). Here is that calculation step by step.

Step 1 · Write the probability as an areaIntegrate the Normal PDF over the interval: $$P(\mu - k\sigma \le X \le \mu + k\sigma) = \int_{\mu - k\sigma}^{\mu + k\sigma} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\, dx$$
Step 2 · Measure in σ's (substitute z)Put \(z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\), so \(x = \mu + \sigma z\) and \(dx = \sigma\, dz\). The limits become \(x = \mu - k\sigma \to z = -k\) and \(x = \mu + k\sigma \to z = k\): $$= \int_{-k}^{k} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\, \sigma\, dz = \int_{-k}^{k} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\, dz$$ The σ from \(dx\) cancels the σ in front, and μ has disappeared. This is why the rule is the same for every Normal: only k is left.
Step 3 · Use symmetry\(e^{-z^2/2}\) is the same at \(z\) and \(-z\), so the left half equals the right half: $$\int_{-k}^{k} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\, dz = 2\int_{0}^{k} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\, dz = 2\Phi(k) - 1$$ (\(\Phi(k)\) is the area to the left of k, which is ½ for the left half plus the area from 0 to k.)
Step 4 · Evaluate the integral\(e^{-z^2/2}\) has no simple antiderivative, but it can be expanded as a series, \(e^{-z^2/2} = 1 - \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^4}{8} - \frac{z^6}{48} + \dots\), and each term integrated. For \(k = 1\): $$\int_0^1 e^{-z^2/2}\, dz = 1 - \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{40} - \tfrac{1}{336} + \tfrac{1}{3456} - \dots \approx 0.8556$$ Multiply by \(\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.7979\): \(\;0.7979 \times 0.8556 \approx 0.6827\). That is the 68%.
Step 5 · Same for k = 2 and k = 3The same calculation (or a z-table, which stores exactly these areas) gives:
\(k = 1:\ 2\Phi(1) - 1 = 2(0.8413) - 1 = 0.6827\)
\(k = 2:\ 2\Phi(2) - 1 = 2(0.9772) - 1 = 0.9545\)
\(k = 3:\ 2\Phi(3) - 1 = 2(0.99865) - 1 = 0.9973\)
Check · HeightsWith \(\mu = 170\) cm and \(\sigma = 8\) cm: 162 to 178 cm is \(z = -1\) to \(1\), so 68.27% of adults. 154 to 186 cm holds 95.45%, and 146 to 194 cm holds 99.73%. Only about 1 adult in 370 is outside that last range.

The rule slice by slice

The same areas can be split into the bands you see in the chart. On each side of the mean: 34.1% between μ and μ + σ, 13.6% between σ and 2σ, 2.1% between 2σ and 3σ, and only 0.1% beyond 3σ. Adding the slices from both sides rebuilds the rule: 34.1 + 34.1 = 68.2; add 2 × 13.6 to get 95.4; add 2 × 2.1 to get 99.6 (the small gaps are rounding). The slices also answer lopsided questions, like "between μ − σ and μ + 2σ" = 34.1 + 34.1 + 13.6 = 81.8%.

Using the rule

Quick estimatesNo table needed for whole σ's. Exam with μ = 70, σ = 10: scoring above 90 is beyond +2σ, so about 5% ÷ 2 = 2.5% of students.
Spotting outliersBeyond ±3σ happens about 3 times in 1,000. A value there is a hint that something unusual (or a mistake) happened.
Only for bell-shaped dataThe numbers come from the Normal curve. An Exponential with mean 1 and σ = 1 has 86.5% within ±1σ, not 68%. For any distribution, Chebyshev only promises at least 75% within ±2σ.

Stage 4

Gamma Distribution

Waiting for the k-th event

The Exponential waits for one event. The Gamma waits for the \(k\)-th. Since the gaps between events are independent Exponentials, a Gamma is just k Exponential waits added together. It is to Exponential what Negative Binomial is to Geometric.

Use it when all of these hold:

1 · Waiting for a fixed number k of eventsLike 3 calls, 10 customers or the 2nd failure.
Breaks if: k isn't fixed in advance.
2 · Events arrive at a steady rate λThe same Poisson-style arrivals as the Exponential.
Breaks if: the rate changes over time.
3 · Independent gapsEach gap between events is a fresh Exponential wait.
Breaks if: one slow gap makes the next one slow too.
4 · Positive, right-skewed totalsTotal times can't be negative, and long tails are expected.
Breaks if: values can be negative, or the data has a long left tail.
Example: a shop gets 2 customers per hour. The time until the 3rd customer is Gamma with \(k = 3,\ \lambda = 2\), so the mean is \(3/2 = 1.5\) hours.

The CDF, via Poisson

"The \(k\)-th event has happened by time \(x\)" means the same as "at least \(k\) events happened in \([0, x]\)". That's a Poisson count with mean \(\lambda x\), so the CDF is one minus the chance of fewer than \(k\) events:

PDF
$$f(x) = \frac{\lambda^k\, x^{k-1}\, e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!}, \quad x \ge 0$$
CDF
$$F(x) = 1 - \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\lambda x)^i}{i!}$$
Summary
$$\mu = \frac{k}{\lambda}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{k}{\lambda^2}$$

k Exponentials, each with mean 1/λ and variance 1/λ², added up.

What each piece means
\(k\)
how many events you wait for (the "shape")
\(\lambda\)
the rate of events per unit time
\(\frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\lambda x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\)
the Poisson chance of exactly k − 1 events in \([0, x]\)…
\(\lambda \times\)
…times the rate: the k-th event lands in the next instant \(dx\) with chance \(\lambda\, dx\)
\((k-1)!\)
equals \(\Gamma(k)\), the number that makes the total area exactly 1

The Gamma also allows non-whole k (written \(\Gamma(k)\) instead of \((k-1)!\)), which makes it a flexible model for any positive, skewed quantity like rainfall or insurance claims. Here we stick to whole k, where the waiting-time story works.

Continuous Uniform

Worked examples

Transport · "more than"

Waiting for the bus

A bus comes every 10 minutes. You arrive at a random time, so your wait is Uniform on [0, 10].

What's the chance you wait more than 7 minutes?

  1. Height of the rectangle: \(\frac{1}{10 - 0} = 0.1\).
  2. The slice from 7 to 10 has width 3.
  3. Area = width × height = \(3 \times 0.1 = 0.3\).
P(X > 7) = 0.30
Deliveries · "between"

The delivery window

A parcel arrives at a uniformly random time between 14:00 and 18:00.

What's the chance it comes between 15:00 and 16:00?

  1. \(X \sim U(14, 18)\), so the height is \(\frac{1}{4} = 0.25\).
  2. The slice 15 → 16 has width 1.
  3. Area = \(1 \times 0.25 = 0.25\). One hour out of four.
P(15 ≤ X ≤ 16) = 0.25
Manufacturing · CDF

Overfilled bottles

A machine overfills each bottle by a uniformly random 2 to 5 ml.

What share of bottles get more than 4.5 ml extra?

  1. CDF: \(F(4.5) = \frac{4.5 - 2}{5 - 2} = \frac{2.5}{3} \approx 0.833\).
  2. "More than" is the complement: \(1 - 0.833 = 0.167\).
P(X > 4.5) ≈ 0.167, about 1 in 6
Exponential

Worked examples

Retail · "within"

The next customer

Customers arrive at a counter at 0.5 per minute on average.

What's the chance the next one arrives within 2 minutes?

  1. \(X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda = 0.5)\).
  2. CDF: \(F(2) = 1 - e^{-0.5 \times 2} = 1 - e^{-1}\).
  3. \(e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), so \(1 - 0.368 = 0.632\).
P(X ≤ 2) ≈ 0.632
Reliability · "lasts longer than"

A light bulb's lifetime

A bulb's lifetime is Exponential with a mean of 2 (thousand hours).

What's the chance it lasts more than 3 thousand hours?

  1. Mean \(= 1/\lambda = 2\), so \(\lambda = 0.5\).
  2. Survival: \(P(X > 3) = e^{-0.5 \times 3} = e^{-1.5}\).
  3. \(e^{-1.5} \approx 0.223\).
P(X > 3) ≈ 0.223
Logistics · "between"

The next delivery

Supply trucks arrive at 1.5 per day on average.

What's the chance the next one comes between day 1 and day 2?

  1. \(X \sim \text{Exp}(1.5)\). Use \(F(2) - F(1)\).
  2. \(= (1 - e^{-3}) - (1 - e^{-1.5}) = e^{-1.5} - e^{-3}\).
  3. \(\approx 0.223 - 0.050 = 0.173\).
P(1 ≤ X ≤ 2) ≈ 0.173

Worked examples

"Try it" converts each question to z-scores and loads the standard Normal (μ = 0, σ = 1). Standardising is exactly how these are solved by hand.

Heights · "taller than"

Over 185 cm

Adult heights are Normal with \(\mu = 170\) cm and \(\sigma = 8\) cm.

What share of adults are taller than 185 cm?

  1. Standardise: \(z = \frac{185 - 170}{8} = 1.875\).
  2. \(P(X > 185) = 1 - \Phi(1.875)\).
  3. \(\Phi(1.875) \approx 0.970\), so \(1 - 0.970 = 0.030\).
≈ 0.030, about 3 in 100
Exams · "between"

Scoring 60 to 85

Exam scores are Normal with \(\mu = 70\) and \(\sigma = 10\).

What share of students score between 60 and 85?

  1. \(z_1 = \frac{60 - 70}{10} = -1\), \(z_2 = \frac{85 - 70}{10} = 1.5\).
  2. \(\Phi(1.5) - \Phi(-1) \approx 0.933 - 0.159\).
  3. \(= 0.775\).
≈ 0.775, about 3 in 4
Quality control · "less than"

Underweight cereal boxes

A machine fills boxes with \(\mu = 500\) g and \(\sigma = 4\) g.

What share of boxes weigh under 492 g?

  1. \(z = \frac{492 - 500}{4} = -2\): two σ below the mean.
  2. \(\Phi(-2) \approx 0.023\). (By 68-95-99.7: 5% lie outside ±2σ, half of that below.)
≈ 0.023, about 1 box in 44
IQ · empirical rule

IQ from 85 to 130

IQ scores are Normal with \(\mu = 100\) and \(\sigma = 15\).

Without a z-table, roughly what share of people score between 85 and 130?

  1. 85 is \(\mu - 1\sigma\) and 130 is \(\mu + 2\sigma\).
  2. Slices: \(\mu - \sigma\) to \(\mu\) is 34.1%, \(\mu\) to \(\mu + \sigma\) is 34.1%, \(\mu + \sigma\) to \(\mu + 2\sigma\) is 13.6%.
  3. \(34.1 + 34.1 + 13.6 = 81.8\%\). The exact value \(\Phi(2) - \Phi(-1)\) is 0.8186.
≈ 0.82, about 4 in 5

Quiz

Normal

Where the bell curve comes from

The formula \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\) looks like it came out of nowhere. It can be built from two simple assumptions, one piece at a time. Each step has a picture of what is going on.

Step 1 · What we are looking forWe want the shape of random errors: misses around a target, measurement noise, deviations from an average. Put the target at 0 and call the density \(f(x)\). Two assumptions, from John Herschel's 1850 argument about errors in star positions:
① The error left/right (x) and the error up/down (y) are independent, and both follow the same \(f\).
② The chance of landing at a spot depends only on its distance from the target, not on its direction.
Step 2 · Throw dartsThink of darts aimed at a bullseye. By ①, the density of hitting \((x, y)\) is \(f(x)\,f(y)\). By ②, that same density depends only on the distance \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), so it is constant on every ring around the centre: $$f(x)\,f(y) = g\!\left(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\right)$$ Put \(y = 0\): \(f(x)\,f(0) = g(|x|)\), so \(g(r) = f(0)\,f(r)\). That gives $$f(x)\,f(y) = f(0)\, f\!\left(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\right)$$ 1,500 darts from the final answer. Every spot on a dashed ring is equally likely (②), and the cloud has the same spread left/right as up/down (①).
Step 3 · Solve it: squares must addDivide both sides by \(f(0)^2\) and take logs. With \(h(x) = \ln\frac{f(x)}{f(0)}\): $$h(x) + h(y) = h\!\left(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\right)$$ So \(h\) turns adding squares (\(x^2 + y^2\)) into adding values. The only smooth function that does this is a multiple of the square: \(h(x) = -c\,x^2\). (Check: \(-cx^2 - cy^2 = -c(x^2 + y^2)\). ✓) The constant is negative (\(c > 0\)) because big misses must be rarer than small ones. Undo the log: $$f(x) = f(0)\, e^{-c x^2}$$ The intuition: on a log scale, a bell is a downward parabola. Each step further out costs more than the last, so the tails die off very fast.
Step 4 · Make the total area 1 (the Gaussian integral)Take \(c = \frac12\) for now (Step 5 shows why). We need \(f(0) = 1/I\), where \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\). This integral has no ordinary antiderivative, but there is a trick: square it and use the dart board. $$I^2 = \int\!\!\int e^{-x^2/2}\, e^{-y^2/2}\,dx\,dy = \int\!\!\int e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy$$ Add the board up ring by ring instead of square by square. A thin ring at radius \(r\) has area \(2\pi r\,dr\), and the height there is \(e^{-r^2/2}\): $$I^2 = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2/2}\; 2\pi r\, dr = 2\pi \Big[-e^{-r^2/2}\Big]_0^{\infty} = 2\pi\,(0 + 1) = 2\pi$$ So \(I = \sqrt{2\pi} \approx 2.5066\), and \(f(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.3989\). The \(\pi\) comes from the circles in the dart board. The extra \(r\) from the ring area is what makes this integral easy: \(r\,e^{-r^2/2}\) is the derivative of \(-e^{-r^2/2}\). The shaded area is exactly 1, and going once round the circle multiplies it by \(2\pi\).
Step 5 · The standard Normal: mean 0, variance 1So far \(\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\). Its mean is 0 by symmetry: in \(\int z\,\varphi(z)\,dz\) the left half (−0.3989) cancels the right half (+0.3989).
Its variance is 1. Integrate by parts with \(u = z\) and \(dv = z\,\varphi(z)\,dz\), so \(v = -\varphi(z)\): $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z^2 \varphi(z)\,dz = \Big[-z\,\varphi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \varphi(z)\,dz = 0 + 1 = 1$$ That is why \(c = \frac12\) was chosen: it makes the spread exactly 1. (In general \(e^{-cz^2}\) has variance \(\frac{1}{2c}\).) Left: \(z\,\varphi(z)\), where the blue and orange areas cancel, so the mean is 0. Right: \(z^2\varphi(z)\), whose total area is the variance, 1.
Step 6 · Shift and stretch to any μ and σAny Normal is the standard one moved and scaled: \(X = \mu + \sigma Z\). Its CDF is $$F(x) = P(\mu + \sigma Z \le x) = P\!\left(Z \le \tfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)$$ Differentiate (chain rule) to get the PDF: $$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\,\varphi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$ That is the full formula. The \(\frac{1}{\sigma}\) has a picture: stretching the bell \(\sigma\) times wider would multiply its area by \(\sigma\), so the height must shrink by \(\sigma\) to keep the area at 1. The mean becomes \(\mu + \sigma\cdot 0 = \mu\) and the variance \(\sigma^2 \cdot 1 = \sigma^2\).
Check · HeightsWith \(\mu = 170\) cm and \(\sigma = 8\) cm, the peak is \(f(170) = \frac{1}{8\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.0499\) per cm: about 5% of adults are within half a centimetre either side of 170 cm. One σ out, at 178 cm, the height is \(0.0499 \times e^{-1/2} \approx 0.0302\), which is 61% of the peak, as for every Normal.

Worked examples

Call centre · "within"

Three calls in an hour

A help line gets 2 calls per hour on average. A trainee needs 3 calls to finish their training.

What's the chance the 3rd call comes within 1 hour?

  1. \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(k = 3,\ \lambda = 2)\). Same as "at least 3 calls in 1 hour", Poisson with mean 2.
  2. \(F(1) = 1 - e^{-2}\left(1 + 2 + \frac{2^2}{2}\right) = 1 - 5e^{-2}\).
  3. \(5e^{-2} \approx 0.677\), so \(F(1) \approx 0.323\).
P(X ≤ 1) ≈ 0.323
Reliability · "lasts longer than"

A part with two spares

A machine part lasts an Exponential time with mean 1 year. You have the part plus 2 spares, so 3 lifetimes in a row.

What's the chance they cover more than 4 years?

  1. Total time \(\sim \text{Gamma}(k = 3,\ \lambda = 1)\).
  2. \(P(X > 4)\) = "fewer than 3 failures in 4 years" \(= e^{-4}\left(1 + 4 + \frac{4^2}{2}\right) = 13e^{-4}\).
  3. \(13 \times 0.0183 \approx 0.238\).
P(X > 4) ≈ 0.238
Bakery · "between"

The 10th customer

Customers come into a bakery at 1.5 per minute. A free cookie goes to the 10th customer of the day.

What's the chance the 10th customer arrives between minute 5 and minute 8?

  1. \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(10,\ 1.5)\), mean \(10/1.5 \approx 6.7\) min.
  2. \(F(8) \approx 0.758\) and \(F(5) \approx 0.224\) (each is a 10-term Poisson sum, so let the chart do it).
  3. \(0.758 - 0.224 = 0.534\).
P(5 ≤ X ≤ 8) ≈ 0.534

Where the Gamma formula comes from

The formula \(f(x) = \frac{\lambda^k x^{k-1} e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!}\) can be built from one picture: events arriving on a timeline at a steady rate \(\lambda\). We only need the Poisson count, which the Exponential stage already linked to these waits.

Step 1 · The k-th event is k gaps added upEvents arrive at rate \(\lambda\). The gaps between them, \(G_1, G_2, \dots\), are independent Exponential(\(\lambda\)) waits (Stage 2). The time of the \(k\)-th event is $$X = G_1 + G_2 + \dots + G_k$$ One run with k = 4 and λ = 1. Each bracket is a fresh Exponential gap, and the orange dot (the 4th event) is X.
Step 2 · Turn "when" into "how many" (the CDF)"The \(k\)-th event has happened by time \(x\)" is the same statement as "at least \(k\) events happened in \([0, x]\)". The count \(N(x)\) of events in \([0, x]\) is Poisson with mean \(\lambda x\), so $$F(x) = P(X \le x) = P(N(x) \ge k) = 1 - \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \underbrace{\frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\lambda x)^i}{i!}}_{p_i(x)}$$ Cut the timeline at any x. If at least k = 4 dots lie to the left, the 4th event has already happened, so X ≤ x. Counting dots (Poisson) answers a waiting-time question.
Step 3 · Differentiate: the sum telescopesThe PDF is the slope of the CDF. Differentiating one Poisson term with the product rule gives two pieces: $$\frac{d}{dx}\, p_i(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\lambda x)^i}{i!} = \lambda\, p_{i-1}(x) - \lambda\, p_i(x) \qquad (p_{-1} = 0)$$ Add these for \(i = 0, 1, \dots, k-1\). Neighbouring terms cancel (\(+\lambda p_0\) against \(-\lambda p_0\), and so on), so only \(-\lambda\, p_{k-1}(x)\) survives. Then \(f = F' = -\sum p_i' = \lambda\, p_{k-1}(x)\): $$f(x) = \lambda \cdot \frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\lambda x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \frac{\lambda^k x^{k-1} e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!}$$
Step 4 · The intuition: k − 1 events, then one moreRead the result back as a story. For the \(k\)-th event to land in a tiny window \([x, x + dx]\), two things must happen:
① exactly \(k - 1\) events by time \(x\): chance \(p_{k-1}(x)\) (Poisson)
② one event in the window: chance \(\lambda\, dx\) (rate × time) $$P(x < X \le x + dx) = p_{k-1}(x) \cdot \lambda\, dx \quad\Rightarrow\quad f(x) = \lambda\, p_{k-1}(x)$$ For k = 4: three dots before x (any positions), then the 4th inside the orange window.
Step 5 · Why (k − 1)!: the total area is 1Substitute \(t = \lambda x\), so \(dt = \lambda\, dx\): $$\int_0^\infty \frac{\lambda^k x^{k-1} e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!}\,dx = \frac{1}{(k-1)!}\int_0^\infty t^{k-1} e^{-t}\,dt = \frac{\Gamma(k)}{(k-1)!}$$ The integral is the Gamma function \(\Gamma(k)\), which gives the distribution its name. Integrating by parts gives \(\Gamma(k) = (k-1)\,\Gamma(k-1)\), and \(\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt = 1\). So \(\Gamma(k) = (k-1)!\), and the area is exactly 1. \(t^{k-1}e^{-t}\) for k = 1, 2, 3, 4. The areas under them are 1, 1, 2, 6, which are 0!, 1!, 2!, 3!. Dividing by \((k-1)!\) scales each one back to area 1.
Step 6 · Mean, variance, peak and the bellSince \(X\) is a sum of \(k\) independent gaps, each with mean \(\frac{1}{\lambda}\) and variance \(\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\), the means and variances add: $$E[X] = \frac{k}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{k}{\lambda^2}$$ The peak is where \(f'(x) = 0\): \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[x^{k-1}e^{-\lambda x}\big] = x^{k-2}e^{-\lambda x}\big((k-1) - \lambda x\big) = 0\), so \(x = \frac{k-1}{\lambda}\), just left of the mean. As \(k\) grows, \(X\) is a sum of many independent waits, so the curve turns into a bell.
Check · Three customersA shop gets \(\lambda = 2\) customers per hour, and we wait for the \(k = 3\)rd. At \(x = 1\) hour: \(f(1) = \frac{2^3 \cdot 1^2 \cdot e^{-2}}{2!} = 4e^{-2} \approx 0.541\) per hour. Step 4 reads the same number as \(\lambda \times p_2(1) = 2 \times \frac{e^{-2}\, 2^2}{2!} = 4e^{-2}\). Mean \(3/2 = 1.5\) hours, peak at \((3-1)/2 = 1\) hour.
